Ask a practice how its marketing is performing and you will usually get a cost per lead. It is a genuinely useful number for the thing it measures, which is the price of a stranger raising their hand. It is a terrible number for the thing it gets used for, which is deciding where next month's budget goes.

The problem is arithmetic, not attitude. Cost per lead divides spend by the count of an event that happens before anyone has been treated, in a business where the gap between the enquiry and the revenue is where most of the variance lives. Two channels can produce identically priced leads and wildly different amounts of delivered care, and nothing in the metric can tell you which is which.

## What the denominator hides

Walk the distance between a lead and a patient and count the places it can end:

- The call is not answered, so the enquiry never becomes a record at all.
- It is answered, but nothing suitable is free within the window the person cares about.
- An appointment is booked and not attended.
- The patient attends, a plan is presented, and it is not accepted.
- The plan is accepted and treatment never starts.

Each of those is a real exit. Channels differ in how their demand behaves at every one of them. Demand from someone searching a specific procedure at eleven at night behaves nothing like demand from a broad awareness campaign, and the difference does not show up until attendance.

So cost per lead systematically flatters whichever channel produces the most enquiries per pound, which is usually the one with the loosest intent. The metric is not wrong. It is answering a question nobody asked.

## Changing the denominator

The correction is not a new attribution model. It is a different divisor: **cost per attended patient**, and where the operation can support it, cost per treatment started.

This is harder, because the numerator lives in advertising systems and the denominator lives in a practice management system or a dispatch platform, and those two were never designed to meet. Which is exactly why so few providers do it, and exactly why the ones who do find the ranking of their channels changes.

In practice the join needs three things:

1. A source recorded at the point of enquiry, including for enquiries that arrive by phone, which is where the volume usually is.
2. An attendance event that comes from the operational system rather than from a booking confirmation. A booking is an intention; attendance is a fact.
3. An honest count of the demand that cannot be traced at all, held as its own figure rather than distributed across the channels that can be.

None of that requires sending patient identifiers anywhere. The join happens inside the governed layer, and what leaves it is an aggregate.

## The uncomfortable part

Recalculating on attendance usually makes the total picture look worse before it looks better. Volumes drop, costs per outcome rise, and someone has to explain to a board that the numbers did not get worse, the measurement got honest.

That conversation is easier if you run it once, deliberately, with both denominators side by side and the difference explained, than if it arrives in pieces over a quarter.

> A cheap lead and a delivered treatment are not the same product. Only one of them pays for the building.

## Where to start

Pick one service line. Join its enquiries to attendance for a single month, by hand if necessary. Compare the two rankings. If they agree, you have lost an afternoon and gained an argument you can retire. If they disagree — and they usually do — you have just found the most valuable measurement work available to you, and you found it before spending anything on it.
