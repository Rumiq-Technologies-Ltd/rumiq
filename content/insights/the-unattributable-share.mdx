Every attribution model has to deal with demand it cannot explain: the caller who says a friend recommended you, the walk-in, the enquiry from a facility relationship that exists entirely in somebody's phone. Models handle this in one of two ways. Either they drop it, or they quietly spread it across the channels they can see.

Both are choices. Neither is usually visible on the report.

## Why spreading is worse than it looks

Redistributing unattributable demand across measured channels is not neutral. It scales up whatever is already being measured, in proportion to how well it is measured. Channels with good instrumentation get credited with demand they did not create, and the channels that produce untrackable demand — word of mouth, referral relationships, the reputation you built over fifteen years — get credited with none of it.

The consequence is directional, and it always points the same way: towards spending more on the things that are easy to measure. If a third of your demand is unattributable and it has been silently allocated to paid search, paid search looks like it is carrying the practice, and the next decision follows from a number that was partly invented.

## Make it a line

The fix is unglamorous. Count it, name it, and put it on the report as its own row:

- **Attributed**: enquiries with a source recorded at the point of contact.
- **Inferred**: enquiries where the source was reconstructed, with the method stated.
- **Unattributable**: enquiries where nobody knows, held at full weight and not redistributed.

The first time this is shown, the share is usually large enough to be uncomfortable, particularly where the phone is the primary channel and call handling records nothing about origin. We are not going to put a figure on it here: the honest answer is that it depends entirely on how enquiries arrive, and a benchmark quoted without a source is exactly the kind of number this piece is arguing against.

Whatever it turns out to be, it is not a failure of the report. It is the report finally describing the operation.

## What changes when you can see it

Three things follow, and none of them require a new tool.

First, the confidence interval around every reallocation decision gets stated out loud. A recommendation to move a fifth of the budget means something different when a third of demand is unexplained, and everyone in the room can now see that.

Second, reducing the unattributable share becomes a project with an owner. It is usually the cheapest measurement work available, because most of it is asking a question at the point of contact and recording the answer in a fixed list rather than free text.

Third, the untrackable channels stop being invisible. Referral relationships that generate real volume can be identified by asking, even when they cannot be tracked, and a relationship you know about is one you can maintain.

> An honest gap is more useful than a confident estimate, because you can shrink a gap.

## The rule

One line, on every report: how much of this we could not explain. Held at full weight, never spread, and trending down over time as the instrumentation improves.

It is the least sophisticated thing on the page and it does more work than the model.
